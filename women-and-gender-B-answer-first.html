Women and Gender in Islam

What does Islam actually teach about women’s role, dignity, and authority?

Last reviewed: July 2026 · 70 resources · reviewed by Yaqeen Research + Ihsan Assurance

Where Yaqeen stands

Yaqeen approaches questions of women and gender with a dual commitment: faithfulness to the Qur’an and Sunnah, and seriousness about the lived experience of Muslim women today. The Qur’an established legal rights for women — to own property, consent to marriage, inherit, and seek divorce — in seventh-century Arabia, centuries before comparable rights appeared elsewhere.

Much of what is presented today as “the Islamic position” on women reflects cultural practice rather than foundational teaching. Our research separates the two: female jurists, hadith scholars, and teachers shaped Islamic intellectual history from its first generation, and that legacy — not its absence — is the standard we measure against.

Key takeaways

  • The Qur’an established enforceable legal rights for women — property, consent, inheritance, divorce — in 7th-century Arabia.
  • Female scholarship is not a modern accommodation: women transmitted hadith, issued fatwas, and taught the scholars of every early generation.
  • Yaqeen’s research holds both commitments at once: fidelity to the textual sources and honesty about how Muslim women actually experience these questions today.
  • Many practices criticized as “Islamic” are cultural imports; distinguishing the two is a core task of this research.

Frequently asked questions

The questions people actually search, answered concisely. Click to expand.

What rights does Islam give women? +
The Qur’an grants women the right to own and manage property, consent to (or refuse) marriage, inherit from family, keep their own earnings, and initiate divorce. These were established as enforceable legal rights in the 7th century. Where Muslim women today are denied them, the cause is cultural practice or weak institutions — not Islamic teaching.
Is hijab required in Islam, or is it cultural? +
Covering is grounded in the Qur’an (24:31, 33:59) and was understood as a religious obligation across the classical schools of law — it is not merely a cultural artifact. What varies culturally is the form it takes. Islam also legislates modesty for men first (24:30), a point often missed in the debate.
Were there female scholars in Islamic history? +
Thousands. Aisha (ra) was among the most prolific transmitters of hadith and a jurist consulted by the Prophet’s companions; al-Shifa bint Abdullah was appointed as a market inspector by Umar (ra); and biographical dictionaries record generations of women who taught the most famous male scholars, including teachers of Ibn Hajar and al-Sakhawi.
Does the Quran treat men and women as equal? +
The Qur’an is explicit that men and women are equal in spiritual worth, moral accountability, and reward (33:35, 4:124). It assigns some differentiated roles and responsibilities — a distinction classical scholars framed as complementarity rather than hierarchy of value. Equality of worth, not sameness of role, is the Qur’anic frame.
What does Islam say about women working or leading? +
Khadijah (ra), the Prophet’s ﷺ first wife, was a successful merchant who employed him; women in the earliest community worked, traded, taught, and held public roles. Classical jurists differed on specific offices, but there is no blanket prohibition on women’s work, scholarship, or leadership in Islamic law.
Can a Muslim woman initiate divorce? +
Yes. Islamic law provides several routes: khulʿ (divorce initiated by the wife), conditions stipulated in the marriage contract, and judicial annulment (faskh) for harm or neglect. The claim that only men can end a marriage misrepresents the fiqh tradition.
Is Islam compatible with feminism? +
It depends on what is meant by feminism. Islam anticipated and exceeds many of the legal protections early feminist movements fought for, and Muslim women can and do advocate for their God-given rights from within the tradition. Where a specific feminist framework treats revelation itself as the problem, the two diverge.
Why does modesty in Islam seem to focus only on women? +
In the Qur’an it doesn’t — the command to lower the gaze and guard modesty is addressed to men first (24:30), before women (24:31). The cultural tendency to police only women’s modesty inverts the Qur’anic order of address and is itself a distortion our research addresses.
